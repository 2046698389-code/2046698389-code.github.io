Shopify bottle deposit apps compared: Upcharge vs Canteen vs FeeBee

Four Shopify apps handle deposits or fees at checkout. For a per-container deposit, test three things in the trial: per-unit quantity, refunds by unit against the original order, and an exportable ledger.

Search the App Store for a Shopify bottle deposit app and you get five results that look similar and are not. Two are general checkout-fee tools with large install bases. Two are deposit tools. One is built for a single national scheme. The review counts are not comparable to each other, because they were earned solving different problems for different merchants.

This page puts the numbers side by side, says plainly where a listing does not state something, and then sets out the trial you can run in twenty minutes to find out which mechanism an app actually uses. Deposit Ledger is my app and it appears in the table; the difference I care about is at the bottom of the page, not in the review count.

On the deposit itself. The amount is set by the scheme in your market, not by the app. In Ireland the two published levels sit at €0.15 and €0.25 across the size bands — confirm the current figures and container scope with Re-turn before configuring anything, and confirm the tax treatment with your accountant. Any app that lets you hard-code a rate without a review date will eventually be wrong.

What each column actually tells you

Reviews

A review count is an install base, not a fit score. 199 reviews on a fee app means 199 merchants wanted a fee at checkout, which is a broad need: service charges, small-order fees, delivery surcharges. It tells you the app is maintained and that support exists. It does not tell you the app can refund a deposit by unit, because most of those 199 reviewers never tried.

Canteen's 3.9 average over 46 reviews is worth reading rather than skipping, though. A smaller sample with a lower average usually means a specific complaint repeating — check whether that complaint is about the thing you need, and whether the responses date from this year.

Price

Not stated here, because listing prices change faster than articles do and a stale number in a comparison table is worse than no number. Two things to look for when you check: the order or transaction cap on the cheap plan, and whether refunds count toward it. A $9 plan that stops at 200 orders a month is a different product from a $19 plan without a cap once you are doing 400.

Per item

This is the column that decides whether the app can do deposits at all. A fee app typically adds a percentage, a flat amount, or a fixed charge per order. A deposit is a count: six 330 ml bottles is six units, one 750 ml bottle is one unit at a higher rate, and the quantity is what the customer gets back later. If the line has no quantity field, there is no per-unit refund either, and the whole model collapses into a manual spreadsheet at month end.

Refund ledger

A ledger means three numbers per period — collected, refunded, outstanding — plus the order-level rows behind them. It is the least published column in this category, and the one that decides whether your month closes. A fee app can be configured to show a refund; it is much rarer for it to keep a running balance per customer, because a fee was never designed to be given back.

Non-Plus

Shopify gives you two mechanisms. A cart line — added by a cart transform or by the app at cart level — works on Basic, Shopify and Advanced, and appears in the cart, the order, the confirmation email and the refund screen. A native custom line rendered inside checkout itself requires Plus and a checkout UI extension. Both can be correct; they are not the same thing, and only one of them is available on the plan most small beverage brands run.

Why a deposit is not a surcharge

The money behaves differently, and that difference lands in three places.

It is a liability, not revenue. A fee is income. A refundable deposit is money you are holding until the container comes back, which is why it usually gets its own tax treatment and its own line in the accounts. Fold it into product revenue and your margin per bottle becomes fiction.

It comes back. Fees do not. Every deposit collected creates an obligation with a quantity attached, and the obligation survives longer than the order — sometimes by a year. An app built around fees has no natural place to store "this customer still holds 14 bottles".

It gets audited by quantity. The question at period end is not "how much did we collect" but "how many containers went out, how many came back, and what is still out there". That is a count, not an amount, and a percentage-based fee line has no way to answer it.

The twenty-minute trial

Install on a development store, not on production, and run this before you look at the pricing page again.

  • Add two container sizes to one cart. Six 330 ml bottles and one 750 ml bottle. A per-item implementation produces the right count at the right rate per band; a fee implementation produces one number with nothing behind it.
  • Check the cart, the checkout, the order and the confirmation email. The customer has to see the deposit to come back for it. If it appears only on the order, expect returns you have no way to attribute.
  • Look at the tax line. The deposit needs its own tax setting, or you are charging tax on money you will give back.
  • Refund four of the six bottles. Deposit line only, quantity four, product line untouched. If stock moves, the app is treating your deposit as a product and the month will not reconcile.
  • Export a month. Collected, refunded, outstanding, with order references. Open the file. If you would not hand it to an accountant, that is your answer.

Do that with two apps and the decision stops being about review counts.

Where Deposit Ledger sits

Deposit Ledger does one job: a per-container deposit, added as its own cart line, refunded by quantity against the original order, and closed out as a month-end ledger you can export. It runs on Basic, Shopify and Advanced because it uses the cart line rather than a checkout extension. It does not file DRS returns, does not transmit anything to a scheme operator, and does not hold your money — the deposit moves through your own payment and refund flow. It calculates and it records; that is the whole product.

If you are already running a fee app and it is doing what you need, keep it. The stores that switch are the ones where the refund screen and the month-end number are the failure — usually somewhere between 50 and 200 orders a month, when the spreadsheet stops being tolerable and a customer asks for four bottles' worth of deposit back and nobody can find the order.

That is the difference worth weighing: not features, not review counts. The ledger and the reconciliation. Every app in the table can put an amount on an order. Fewer can tell you, in February, what you still owe in deposits and to whom.

Frequently asked

Which Shopify app is best for bottle deposits?

Whichever one survives the trial above — the answer depends on whether you need a fee or a refundable deposit per container. Test per-unit quantity, a refund by unit against the original order, and an exportable collected/refunded/outstanding ledger. If all three work, the review count is a detail; if the third one fails, nothing else compensates.

Do these apps require Shopify Plus?

A cart line works on Basic, Shopify and Advanced; a native custom line inside checkout needs Plus. Ask each app which mechanism it uses before installing, because the answer decides whether the deposit appears in the checkout summary or only in the cart, the order and the refund screen — and whether the app is usable on your plan at all.

Can I use a general fee app to charge a bottle deposit?

You can collect the money. What you usually do not get is the refund by unit and the running balance, because a fee line is modelled as revenue from the start. If your customers never bring containers back, a fee app is the simpler choice; if they do, the refund path is the requirement and it is worth testing before you commit.
